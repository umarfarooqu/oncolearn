FAQ SECTION

The Questions Women Ask the Most

Hover over any question to reveal detailed answers from breast cancer health resources.

What is breast cancer?
Breast cancer is a disease where cells in the breast tissue grow out of control, forming a tumor. If left untreated, these cells can spread (metastasize) to other parts of the body. While most commonly found in women, it can affect anyone with breast tissue.
What are the earliest warning signs?
Early signs can include a new lump in the breast or underarm, unexplained swelling, dimpling or puckering of the breast skin, changes in the nipple (such as turning inward), or unusual nipple discharge other than breast milk.
How often should I do a breast self-exam?
It is highly recommended to perform a breast self-exam once a month. The best time is usually a few days after your menstrual cycle ends, when breasts are least tender. Knowing your "normal" is the best way to notice any changes early.
When should I start getting mammograms?
Most medical guidelines recommend women should have the choice to start annual breast cancer screening with mammograms at age 40. By age 45, regular annual mammograms are strongly advised. Women with a higher risk profile may need to start earlier.
Does a family history mean I will definitely get it?
No. While having a first-degree relative (mother, sister, daughter) with breast cancer roughly doubles your risk, the reality is that about 85% of women who develop breast cancer have no family history of the disease.
What are the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes?
Everyone has BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes; their job is to repair cell damage and keep breast cells growing normally. However, inherited mutations (abnormalities) in these genes significantly increase the risk of developing breast and ovarian cancers.
Can lifestyle changes lower my risk?
Yes. While you cannot change genetics or age, maintaining a healthy weight, exercising regularly, limiting alcohol consumption, and avoiding long-term postmenopausal hormone therapy can actively reduce your overall risk.
Are breast cysts the same as breast cancer?
No. Breast cysts are fluid-filled sacs within the breast that are usually benign (non-cancerous). They are very common, especially in women before menopause. However, any new lump or mass should always be evaluated by a healthcare professional.
Can men get breast cancer?
Yes. Although it is rare and accounts for only about 1% of all breast cancer diagnoses, men do have breast tissue and can develop the disease. The signs, symptoms, and treatments are very similar to those in women.
What are the most common treatment options?
Treatment is highly personalized based on the cancer's stage and biological characteristics. It typically involves a combination of surgery (lumpectomy or mastectomy), radiation therapy, chemotherapy, hormone therapy, or targeted drug therapies.
